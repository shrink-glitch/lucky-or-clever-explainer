Lucky or clever?

An interactive guide to Gerstenberg, Ullman, Nagel, Kleiman-Weiner, Lagnado & Tenenbaum (2018), "Lucky or clever? From expectations to responsibility judgments", Cognition 177, 122-141. The paper argues that how much we credit or blame someone depends on two things: what their action reveals about the kind of person they are, and how close their action was to making the difference to the outcome.

The puzzle

Most theories say people are held more responsible when they act unexpectedly. But Johnson & Rips (2015) found people were sometimes held more responsible for positive outcomes of expected, optimal choices.

The paper resolves this: whether an unexpected action earns more or less credit depends on whether it is diagnostic of good or bad future performance.

Two processes

  • Dispositional inference (person-centred): from the action and outcome, infer what type of agent this is, then ask how much our expectation of their future success has changed relative to our prior.
  • Causal attribution (action-centred): how close was the action to being pivotal, i.e. would the outcome have been different had they chosen otherwise?

Three agent types

  • Average: chooses using only the base-rate probabilities.
  • Skilled: can read a cue about what will actually happen this time, and uses it correctly.
  • Unskilled ("bad"): thinks they can read the cue but uses it the wrong way round, so does worse than chance.

All three choose noisily (softmax, fitted β = 1.5).

The model (Eq. 10)

Responsibility = α
  + w1 × (difference in expected future reward)
  + w2 × (pivotality)

Fitted α = 6, w1 = 28, w2 = 28 (on normalised predictors). The equal weights mean participants cared about both factors about equally. Pivotality = 1/(N+1), where N is the minimal number of changes needed to make the action pivotal.

Experiment 1 vs. Experiment 2

In Experiment 1 (agent types not described), goalies got more credit for unexpected saves, while spinner players got less credit for unexpected wins. In Experiment 2, once participants were told skilled spinner players exist, spinner credit became roughly flat across probabilities. A cluster analysis showed why: one subgroup (35% of the spinner group, 22% of the goalie group) stayed sceptical about skill and favoured expected wins, while the other credited unexpected wins more. Blame rose with how unexpected the action was in both worlds and both experiments, and both blame and credit were higher overall in the goalie world.

Gardener: did the choice make a difference? (Experiment 3)

A gardener picks one of two fertilizers: one makes the flower grow 70% of the time, the other 30%. Participants were also told what would have happened with the other fertilizer. If the outcome would have been the same either way, the choice was not pivotal (pivotality = 1/2, since one change would have made it pivotal). Values are the paper's data (Tables C1 and C2).

Key takeaways

1. No direct link from "unexpected" to responsibility

An unexpected success earns more credit when it signals skill (goalies, gardeners) and less when it looks like a lucky bad decision (spinner players in Experiment 1). What matters is what the action predicts about future performance.

2. Unexpected failures are blamed more - everywhere

Across all worlds, failing after an unlikely choice is diagnostic of an unskilled agent, so blame rises the less likely the chosen action was to succeed.

3. Pivotality matters on top of inference

Gardeners were blamed and credited more when their choice actually made the difference, even where participants' inferences about the agent were similar - and this effect was larger for unexpected choices.

4. Both components are needed

Across Experiments 2-3 the full model fit well (cross-validated r = .86, BIC 158.6) and beat a difference-only model (r = .70), a pivotality-only model (r = .63), and an optimality model in the style of Johnson & Rips (r = .66).

5. People differ in how plausible they think skill is

The flat average credit in the Experiment 2 spinner world hides two subgroups with opposite patterns, each consistent with its own agent inferences.

6. Limits

Achievement contexts only, agents differing only in skill, a single action per person, and online US participants (MTurk). The comparison standard (this person vs. a reasonable person) was not tested, and moral character was not modelled.
