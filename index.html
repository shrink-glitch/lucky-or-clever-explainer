Lucky or clever?

An interactive simplifier for the paper “Lucky or clever? From expectations to responsibility judgments”. The paper argues that responsibility judgments depend on two things: what an action reveals about a person, and whether the action actually made a difference.

The paper’s core question

When someone’s action leads to a good or bad outcome, how much credit or blame should they receive?

A surprising puzzle: sometimes people get more responsibility for unexpected actions, but sometimes they get less. This page simplifies why.

Two mental ingredients

  • Expectation change: What does the action reveal about the person? Are they skilled, average, or unskilled?
  • Pivotality: Did the action actually make a difference to the outcome?

Simplified formula

Responsibility ≈ expectation change + pivotality

In the actual paper, the model is more formal and quantitative, but the core intuition is this: we judge people by what their action teaches us about them, and by how causally important their action was.

Key takeaways

1. Unexpected does not always mean more responsibility

The paper resolves a puzzle: unexpected actions can receive more responsibility when they reveal skill, but less when they reveal poor judgment or luck.

2. Credit depends on what the action reveals

If an unexpected success makes the person look skilled, they get more credit. If it just looks like luck, they get less credit.

3. Blame increases when unexpected actions fail

If someone takes a risky or unexpected action and fails, observers often infer poor decision-making or lack of skill, increasing blame.

4. Causal impact matters

People are held more responsible when their action was pivotal — when the outcome would have been different if they had chosen differently.

5. Responsibility combines person and action

The paper’s model combines a person-centered process — updating expectations about the agent — with an action-centered process — evaluating causal pivotality.
